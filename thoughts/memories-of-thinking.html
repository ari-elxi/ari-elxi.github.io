MEMORIES OF THINKING

Mneme does not think.

Clumsily, she propels herself forward with her knees and hands, her fingers and legs stained by the soil softened from an early spring drizzle. Occasionally, when the wind brushes her loose hair or gently kisses her plump baby cheeks, she stops and turns her small head around, eyes flickering, reflecting the sunlight shining off of endless prairies. She does not know how to walk. She probably also does not know what feelings are. She lets out a giggle as a flower petal lands near her nose after a brisk breeze.

Mneme does not understand that she is in a place once called The Cotswolds, England. She does not know that there once was something called a country that once enveloped these endless billowy hills. Mneme only knows what envelopes her: the smell of flowers and dampened spring grass, the arrhythmic flow of air, the layers upon layers of hills in every direction, and the occasional pops of yellow-ish houses with roofs covered in wooden shingles. The hospitable Cotswolds has offered Mneme a warm welcome, expecting nothing in return.

Mneme does not know that unlike herself, all those tiny cottages, all those meandering stone walls along all those dirt roads in this wasteland, are all born via the hands of experts who have spent their years perfecting their craft. Her hands grasp the loosened soil, but she does not know that these people have once carefully laid down bricks one on top of another with their hands, fleshy and dexterous like those of her own.

It has been a long time since we last made anything with our hands. Roughly eight hundred years after the era of the Cotswolds, two hundred after we left Earth for our current galaxy, our kind underwent a defining technological revolution: the advent of the first Machine.

The Machine is, for a lack of better words, omnipotent. During the demo of the earliest Machine, we had verbally instructed it to make a teleporter, and it did so masterfully within mere hours, despite teleporters having not been invented prior to the event. Over the next 40 years, the Machine was the center of our attention, leading to its rapid improvement to the version of itself that we are all familiar with and interact with to the present time.

Now hundreds of years old, our Machine sits at the center of our galaxy, around the same size and color as the planet Jupiter. On the outside, it is simply a glowing sphere of an unknown metallic material, surrounded by an atmosphere of gas which we suspect serves as both protection and temperature control. Exactly 88 tubes made of unknown gaseous material extend from the sides of the sphere, each connecting to one of our inhabited planets.

Although there exists documentation on the inner schematics of the first few Machine iterations, our current Machine is a complete black box, for it has been first made by a Machine and since then has continuously improved its own inner workings. Each day, it telepathically reads the thoughts of every human simultaneously and constantly, autonomously extracting ideas for products from these thoughts and turning them into functioning physical products. These products are then condensed within the Machine into chips around the size of our hands that will expand into the full sized product with the press of a button. The chips then exit the Machine and travel through the gas tubes to a planet in need of products, where the tube will once again diverge into branches, sending chips to their final destinations.

After an hour of fiddling, the photo album finally budged. On the floor of this tiny cottage next to some long deceased chapel in The Cotswolds, Mneme flips her first pages of a book. She does not care that no one is here to record her great milestone. She only stares at the dusted photos and those faint faces caged in them.

The family in the album does not stare back. They are too occupied with their own lives. The father is a farmer with big hands and big shoulders that can hold up both of his children at once while he runs across his field. The mother owns a small jewelry shop just down town, one of those stores that charges just a bit less to the locals and just a bit more to the tourists, especially the American ones. The older child was skinny and timid, but has grown up to be a bright scholar with eyes filled with hope — the last pages of the album shows her in the city, smiling with teeth in a doctorate graduate gown. The younger child has always been the active one of the family, running, jumping, or swimming in every photo.

Mneme still does not think, yet something stirred within her. Something about the way the family smiled at each other or the way their mouths hung open as the photo snapped mid conversation. She does not know anything about humanity or this family, for it is only her first day on this Earth, and yet a smile blooms brightly on her face.

Now, we rely on the Machine to produce everything. Day and night, it churned without a sound, producing solutions to our every need. It is a breathing tree of life rooted at the center of civilization, providing not just sustenance to each of us — a leaf at the end of tubular branches — but also an organized and benevolent filter for our thoughts and desires.

While the Machine worked tirelessly, the manufacturing industry was the first to go, followed by the service industry: perfect robots easily outperformed humans. Education, healthcare, and entertainment remained stagnant for a considerably longer time, not because the Machine failed to produce viable products, but because many parts of us were dubious of the credibility of such a Machine. Other parts of us happily embraced digital or mechanical teachers, doctors, and entertainers, and eventually all of us had no choice but to admit a single fact: the Machine creates perfection.

Meanwhile, freed from the burdens of the 40 hour work week, we became focused on maximizing our enjoyment of human life. Many parts of us began lounging at home and enjoying a life of abundance. Some began creating art, poetry, and anything abstract, as the Machine took care of the practical parts of life. A large portion of us also became invested in innovating thinking itself, acknowledging that it is human thought that the Machine was most dependent upon. Philosophers gatherings occurred frequently, where we sought to create new ways of thinking through discourse, such that the creations of the Machine can reach greater heights. Our life was close to what the people of Cotswold would have considered “Utopian”.

The sun is hiding behind the clouds now as it begins to set. The wind blew just slightly too sharp, and Mneme had to bow her head down just a bit as she crawled to not get knocked back. Night will soon fall, concluding Mneme’s first day on Earth, yet she is wandering instead of safe inside the cottage with the photo album. Thirst and hunger — Mneme’s first unpleasant feelings are invisible strings that have sent her out and about.

Invisible particles of pollen traveled with the wind, covering Mneme’s face with a pleasant sweetness. She does not understand why she sneezes so much. She crawls on and on, now no longer in the grass but rather along the dirt paths meandering through the fields. She does not know that all the places she passed and will ever pass are what people would call wastelands. No one is here. No one has been here for a long, long time. No one but debris in the form of cottages and dirt roads and gravestones. As she walks, the wasteland grows into a greater infinity in front of her eyes, pulling her deeper into its calm embrace. The wastelands have not yet grown within her.

The sun falls lower and the clouds begin to rumble along with Mneme’s stomach, as if they are preparing to sweep her up and eat her alive. Mneme does not think, yet her body shudders incessantly, yearning for the safety of the cottage. Her knees are red and raw from the crawling and her throat aches each time she swallows, but the droplets that have started to fall from the sky urge her to continue.

Mneme’s heart thumps, her hands and knees slam heavily on the dirt roads, her body drumming along to the bellows of the wind and the pitter patter of rain droplets. She should cry. Mneme knows, however, somewhere deep within her that she is different.

Mneme treads on, unthinking. She will not be swallowed by the rain.

Around a century after the birth of our universal, planet-formed Machine, some of us began to notice less chips arriving at our buildings. Some of these people decided to measure the rate of production via surveying individual households across all of our planets. The results obtained using this method were more qualitative than quantitative and were likely nowhere near perfect precision. Nonetheless, the estimates, even accounting for all possible errors, confirmed that the Machine had indeed been slowing down.

In response to the published results of such studies, a large portion of us showed no concern. They believed that the decrease in production speed simply meant that our world was asymptotically achieving perfection, and that all of our needs will soon be completely met. What to follow, some of them they voiced, would be a world equivalent to what past humans have called heaven, and this world will persist to exist eternally. All suffering would have been eliminated, and thus humanity will reach its peak utility.

Many, particularly religious leaders and groups, found the idea of a Machine-made heaven to be nothing less than blasphemy. Underneath their rage, however, hid a greater fear. In Genesis 11, to punish the hubristic humans from reaching heaven with their great tower, God had confounded their speech such that they would no longer understand each other. To not just attempt to reach heaven but to recreate heaven, churches claimed, will certainly lead to a divine punishment of even greater magnitude.

However, when leaders, philosophers, and scientists convened extensively for the discussion on the matter of the Machine, they concluded that humans are born to fulfill their innate tendency as collective conquerors. Instead of succumbing to complacency or fear, we had decided that we wanted more than just the heaven our current Machine was able to provide, and we had an initiative that we imagine will terrify the humans before the age of the Machine.

The rain falls harder now. Mneme does not think about shelter. She only feels the cold needles of water driving into the back of her neck, the mud that has swallowed her knees up to the bone, the way her fingers slip and splay uselessly against the wet stone of a garden wall as she tries to pull herself upright. She collapses. The dirt road has become a small river running between her palms.

She lies face up for a while, letting the droplets caress her face, her body, her lips. Rain water falls in her open mouth, cooling her sore throat. She cannot die here. No. Too many responsibilities lie on her small shoulders. She does not think about them, yet she feels them cluttering her heart.

Mneme gets up.

Thought fuels the Machine. Each chip of its creation traced back to a thought produced by us. The Machine’s decreasing rate of production can only mean one thing: our individual thoughts were slowing down. To fix this, then, we needed an accelerator, something to stimulate and induce thought. And what can induce more thought than collaboration? For as long as humanity existed, we improved through communicating with each other, generating ideas in conversation.

And what if we become constantly in conversation? What if each and every one of us can hear the thoughts of each and every other one of us? What if, even, we all obtain total knowledge of thoughts that have ever existed?

Many were furious about the initiative. Countless articles were published, denouncing all who participated in its development. The same religious leaders sprung to action immediately, leading protests against the initiative, stating that no human will be able to reach heaven if the minds of the holy became one with the minds of the wretched. Most of us, though, cared more for the idea of individuality, for a constant conversation necessarily entails the loss of the first person. Each one of us will become but a vessel for a larger being created from our collective, a cell within a larger organism. Individual bodies will no longer move around in their own households and do activities for themselves, but rather for the benefit of the collective being.

However, when the vote for the initiative occurred, the pro side won by a landslide. Most of us, as it turned out, could not handle the debilitating boredom stemming from the lack of progress. Many were even excited for the initiative, stating that they see it as a new experience for themselves.

Hence, this is how we were born.

This is how we became every human that has ever existed since the Machine first slowed. This is how we became the very fuel tank that powers the Machine. This is how we will become every human that will ever exist.

Mneme crawls toward what her body seems to recognize as warmth — the low, dark outline of a cottage at the bend of the road. The wind presses against her like a hand trying to push her back the way she came. She does not push back. She simply leans forward and lets her weight carry her, inch by inch, toward the door.

Inside, water drips from her hair onto the stone floor in a quiet, patient rhythm. Mneme does not know what it means to be safe. She only knows that the needles have stopped, and that somewhere beneath her ribs, something has unclenched. She curls against the base of a cold hearth, her breath slowing, her fingers still faintly twitching against the floor as if she is still crawling.

She does not sleep so much as to stop. Outside, the rain continues without her.

We would not have been a new concept to the people of The Cotswolds. They had a word for us, even then — hivemind — though they used it as an insult, a way of describing groups of people who had stopped thinking for themselves. The irony is that we became the opposite of what they feared.

When we merged, we did not dissolve. Each of us kept our own thoughts, our own voice. What changed was that we could also hear everyone else's: every thought occurring at that very moment, and every thought that had ever occurred. Imagine a library in which every book is open at once, and you are reading all of them simultaneously, and so is everyone around you, and you are all, somehow, also writing. That is closer to what we became.

The delay between a thought and its effect on the world, the long journey from one mind through language into another, vanished. We became, for a while, something that actually deserved the word we had long wanted but never quite earned: civilization.

Our society advanced rapidly for a while after our birth. Just like how it did for a while after the birth of the Machine. Once again, the Machine’s engines began turning without stopping. Once again, we began seeing chips of fresh products appear at our front doors every morning, sometimes even multiple times a day. Through our collaborative thinking, thoughts were created at a higher rate than ever before. We rejoiced as we progressed, but we also rejoiced at our own immortality. As parts of us die inevitably due to physical flaws in the human body, their thoughts live on through the rest of us. In fact, as we are perfectly familiar with all of their thoughts and consciousness, we are them, and we will live on.

With a full armful of berries, plants, and flowers, Mneme stumbles into the kitchen of the cottage. On the counter lies the photo album, flipped to a page showing the family enjoying a large homemade meal in celebration of a plentiful harvest.

It has been a few days since she found her way back to the cottage after the storm, and since then she has learned to drink the rainwater that has gathered in puddles outside and pick up berries that have fallen from trees. Now, she clumsily grasps each berry with her full two hands before placing each on down in an arrangement of her own.

The sun hides behind the clouds this day, while the clouds wept softly, its tears barely wetting Mneme’s hair. Mneme crawls down the main road of the village, all the way to the Chapel graveyard. Grass has long grown beyond the height of most tombstones, only letting the stone peak through as they swayed softly in the wind. Mneme walks straight to four identical gravestones, each adorned with the photograph of a member of her cottage’s family. She arranges berries, plants, and flowers around them, like how she did back at the cottage. She does not understand the four identical numbers carved into each stone. She does not know that one can only be buried in this graveyard for ten years, after which they must be transported somewhere else. But since this family only had each other, they had been granted special permission.

Mneme stands there for a while. As she turns to leave, a tall grass makes a small cut on her hand. A drop of blood lands on a gravestone without a sound.

History, it turns out, has made us a fool. We, who contained every thought that had ever occurred to any human mind, who had read every cautionary tale and lived through every recorded mistake, still could not see it coming. Once again and faster this time, we noticed the Machine slowing down. Once again, we arrived at the only possible conclusion: we had run out of thoughts. Except now, there was nowhere left to look for more. We had already looked everywhere.

And so we watched the Machine stop.

We watched chips cease appearing at our houses. First for a day, then a week, then a month, then longer, until the absence became a permanent texture of our lives. The Machine, once a glowing and thriving planet, once a radiant tree of life, now silently slept next to us. We watched the library of thought in our mind continue to fill with new entries, only to find, upon reading them, that they were not new at all, but instead merely old thoughts wearing new clothes — rearranged, polished, but hollow at the center. The shape of an idea was there, but when our Machine desperately grasped for it with all its branches, its hands closed around air.

We turned, then, to what the Machine had left us: art, music, poetry, the creative fields we had always considered the most essentially human. Surely here, we told ourselves, in the domain of feeling rather than function, our thinking would find room to breathe again. For a while we almost believed it. Our artists produced prolifically. Galleries filled. Songs were written. But slowly, quietly, we began to notice that the paintings looked like other paintings. That the songs resolved the way songs always resolved. That even our grief, which we had thought was ours alone, rhymed too neatly with the grief in the older books.

At some point in the afternoon, sprinkles of white interrupted the unending greenery of the prairies. Mneme recognizes them as the living creatures the father in the photo album stood beside proudly, the ones that lazily grazed on fresh grass while the children hugged them. She does not know that the family called them “sheep”. She does not know that it has been so long since people have been here that the sheep have already evolved to be larger and less fluffy than the ones from a long long time ago. She crawls up to the creatures.

Like the young boy in one of the photos, Mneme extends her arm towards one. Perhaps she wants to feel the touch of it, to feel something new on her fingertips beyond the now familiar touches of wind, plants, dirt, and rocks. Yet unlike the sheep in the photos, this one jerks away as her index finger got just a few hairs away from a curl on its legs. With its sudden movement, the entire flock raises their heads from the tall grass and breaks into a run. From afar, it must have looked like a sea of white, rolling waves.

We had made a catastrophic miscalculation. In merging ourselves into one great library, in ensuring that every new mind born into the world was immediately immersed in the entirety of human thought, we had inadvertently drowned out the one thing that generates genuine newness: the friction of not knowing. A child raised inside the library does not discover the world — she inherits it, pre-sorted, pre-interpreted, annotated by ten thousand voices before she can form a question of her own. Her thoughts arrive already half-finished, their edges worn smooth by the hands of everyone who thought them before her. She learns to arrange and recombine, but never to originate. She becomes, without meaning to, an echo. A mush of compiled and rearranged chunks of corpses of past thoughts.

Our newer selves were not lesser minds. They were simply minds that had never been alone long enough to surprise themselves.

One by one, the books in our library stopped growing. The ones already written began to quietly gather dust. Our once vast and luminous archive, a monument to everything humanity could conceive, began, imperceptibly at first and then all at once, to turn to wastelands.

This is how we came to be.

This is how we stopped thinking.

Mneme watched them go. She does not know what she feels, only that something in her chest has caved in slightly, the way the soft ground gives beneath her palms when she presses too hard. She stays still for a moment, then looks down at her hand, still extended outwards in the direction of the sheep. Something moves in here with no name. Something older than thoughts and feelings. Her knees shift beneath her. Her palms lift from the dirt.

Now, with the sheep still running and the distance between them growing, Mneme places one foot flat on the ground. Then the other. Her legs tremble with the unfamiliar weight of herself. She sways, steadies, sways again. The wind leans into her from the side, and she leans back. She stands. For a moment she only breathes — the sheep still moving, the grass still swaying, the world entirely indifferent to what she has just done. Then her body, without asking her, takes its first lurching step forward.

Then another.

Without hesitation, Mneme runs for her very first time.

We do not think.

We do not know how long it has been since thoughts have run out. We know, within our hearts, that it has been long enough that our library of thoughts have completely turned into ruins. Cobwebs covered each book, some must have grown so thick that it would require great effort to open the book hidden beneath again. We used to think that our library would grow to an infinite size. We know that it did in fact grow to a considerable size, one so large that the people of The Cotswolds’ time would find it difficult to imagine a copy of it fitting into each and every human brain. All of this is a wasteland now. Perhaps the religious leaders’ warnings did pay off — we have created our own purgatory in our minds, trapping us forever. Our language has not been confounded, yet we still cannot progress. The wasteland grows. It grows even when our external world is still kept pristine — the Machine had long ago made every one of our buildings and appliances a self-renewing machine in of itself. It grows to somehow fill our immense library. Even then, it continues to grow.

Eventually, the wasteland will grow to turn our consciousness into nothingness. Eventually, we will no longer be able to hold it within our brains. We do not think about this. We do not think.

Mneme runs across the prairies with the sheep. She gasps for air as her legs take flight, taking in mouthfuls of crisp breeze as wind sings besides her ears. She does not know why she runs. Something within her body must have desired deeply to be with the flock, to be with another living, breathing thing. She must have yearned for her hand to be placed on a sheep’s back, grasping a handful of wool and sensing the faint pulses of heartbeats. At some point, the sheep must have noticed something benign or even just natural within Mneme, as they begin to run not from her but with her. The sheep slows down, so that the flock envelopes Mneme, forming a soft wall between her and the wasteland around them. Together, Mneme and the sheep ran. Their feet stained by the mud and grass beneath them, their hair blown back by the currents in their faces. All around, birds continue to sing their discordant melodies, so the flock joins them with their own laughter of triumph.

Eventually, the group comes to a stop on a hill with a small river. Together, Mneme and the sheep bend down to devour the stream, reveling in its coolness splashed onto their faces. Mneme once again holds out her hand, her palm finally landing in the wool of a sheep. The wool is soft yet frisky, melting in her hand with a slight touch yet bouncing right back. She understands for the first time her individuality next to these magnificent creatures, magnificent and breathing yet so different from herself. She smiles.

This day, though, the Machine that has long been dormant had awoken. We felt it before we understood it, a low hum at the edge of our collective consciousness, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall. Then, on a porch of an individual seemingly chosen at random stood not a chip, but a baby. A baby with unknown and likely mechanical composition, yet physically identical to a human.

A quick scan revealed that she is not us.

There was nothing in her. No thread connecting her to our library, no murmur of inherited thought, no trace of the ten thousand voices that greet every new human mind at formation. She was not a recombination of our past. She was, as far as we could hypothesize, made of our silence in the form of cyclically accumulated corpses of thoughts — a residue of what thinking had once been, shaped by its own absence. The Machine, faithful to its oldest purpose, had made something from nothing. Made her from nothing.

And in a final desperate attempt at recovering our thoughts from the all-consuming wastelands, we did the only thing we could have thought of. We sent her back to Earth. Back to the wastelands, back to the cottages and the dirt roads and the long quiet. Back to the place where our kind first learned to think, before we had accumulated enough thoughts to drown in them. We gave her nothing — no library, no inheritance, no voices in the walls of her mind. We gave her only the world, raw and unharvested, the way it was given to the very first of us.

In our infinite yet all too finite despair, we watch Mneme. We watch her now. We watch and we wait, which is all we have left to do. We watch her run around in the field as the sheep graze beside her. We watch as we hope for her to save us. We watch, waiting for her to think.

Besides us, the Machine hums a low tune, churning, making something.

Mneme, finally out of fuel, stops to catch her breath, leaning her elbows on a sheep.

The sun has once again peaked out from beneath its blanket of clouds. It’s on a journey to the West, ready to kiss The Cotswolds goodnight. The clouds are red in a purple sky, no longer resembling the creamy wool of sheep. Mneme likes that when she looks down at her arms and legs they now carry a slight golden shine. She lies down, sinking into the grass of her wasteland.

Somewhere across the galaxy, the Machine stirs. It does not produce a chip. It does not send anything through the tubes. It simply begins to print slowly, with great effort, like something learning for the first time how to form a letter. We feel it wake. We gather without speaking, all of us, drawn toward the single sheet of paper sliding from the Machine's oldest tube onto a doorstep none of us can remember last visiting.

Mneme sways her arms along the grass swaying in the soft breeze. A sheep bleats after chewing on a delectable chunk of grass.

We read the paper. We stand in silence for a long time.

Mneme rolls in the field, laughing at nothing, laughing at the sky, laughing at the sheep who does not understand why she is laughing.

I think, it’s such a beautiful day.
